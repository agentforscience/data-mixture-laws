The composition of a pre-training corpus is chosen mostly by heuristics.
Prior data mixing laws predict validation loss from domain proportions, but they do not tell us whether downstream accuracy follows the same shape.
We test a concrete hypothesis: at a fixed model size and token budget, performance is log-linear in each domain's share and saturates near 20\%, code is the strongest driver of reasoning scores ($r > 0.7$), and more than 50\% web text hurts knowledge tasks.
We train 81 small GPT models (17M parameters, 60M tokens each) from scratch on 53 controlled mixtures of web text, Wikipedia, code, and scientific papers, with seed replicates at 14 mixtures.
The hypothesis holds for language-modeling loss and essentially nowhere else.
Held-out loss on a domain is log-linear in that domain's share ($R^2$ of 0.98--0.9996, against 0.67--0.87 for a linear fit), but it does not saturate at 20\%: loss keeps falling by 0.36--0.55 nats between 20\% and 100\%.
Downstream accuracy is nearly flat across mixtures, with most differences inside seed noise.
Code share does not correlate with our reasoning composite ($r = -0.10$ on 16 random mixtures), and 75\% web text is indistinguishable from 25--50\% on knowledge probes.
The one robust downstream effect is that a pure-code corpus costs 7.4 points of fine-tuned question-answering accuracy.
At this scale, a log-share law fitted on about thirty runs predicts loss on unseen mixtures, but these loss differences do not turn into measurable downstream differences.
